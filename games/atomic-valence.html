1. Atomic Number (Z)

The atomic number is the number of protons in the nucleus of an atom. It uniquely defines an element.

Protons = Z (Atomic Number) — ALWAYS. Never changes for a given element.

2. Mass Number (A)

The mass number is the total number of protons and neutrons in the nucleus.

Mass Number (A) = Protons (Z) + Neutrons (N)
Neutrons (N) = A − Z
Note: Elements exist as isotopes (same Z, different N). This game uses the most abundant naturally occurring isotope (or the most stable isotope for synthetic elements). Therefore, N is fixed for each element in this game.

3. Electrons in a Neutral Atom

A neutral atom has no net charge. The positive charge of protons is exactly balanced by the negative charge of electrons.

Electrons (neutral) = Protons = Z
Example: Carbon has Z = 6. Therefore, a neutral carbon atom has 6 protons, 6 neutrons (A=12), and 6 electrons.

Ions — Charged Species

When an atom loses or gains electrons, it becomes an ion. The number of protons never changes; only the number of electrons changes.

Cation — Positive Ion

Formed when a metal atom loses one or more electrons. The ion has more protons than electrons, so the net charge is positive.

Electrons in Cation = Z − (positive charge magnitude)
Example: Na⁺ (Sodium ion, charge +1)
Z = 11 → Protons = 11
Electrons = 11 − 1 = 10
It lost 1 electron to achieve a stable noble-gas configuration (like Neon).

Anion — Negative Ion

Formed when a non-metal atom gains one or more electrons. The ion has more electrons than protons, so the net charge is negative.

Electrons in Anion = Z + (negative charge magnitude)
Example: O²⁻ (Oxide ion, charge −2)
Z = 8 → Protons = 8
Electrons = 8 + 2 = 10
It gained 2 electrons to complete its octet (like Neon).
Critical Rule: Protons are always exactly equal to Z. Neutrons are always A − Z. Only electrons change! When you see a charge on the symbol, immediately ask: "Did it lose (cation) or gain (anion) electrons?"

Valence Shell

The valence shell is the outermost shell of an atom that contains electrons. It is identified by the principal quantum number n (1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7).

Valence Shell = highest principal quantum number (n) that contains electrons in the ground-state configuration.
Important: For transition metals, the (n−1)d subshell is being filled, but the outermost principal shell is still n (the s-shell). For example, Iron (Fe, Z=26) has configuration [Ar] 3d⁶ 4s². The outermost principal shell is n = 4, even though the 3d subshell is also incomplete.

Valence Electrons

The valence electrons are the electrons present in the valence shell of an atom. In this game, we use the basic school-level definition:

  • s-block and p-block (main group): Valence electrons = total electrons in the outermost shell (highest n).
  • d-block (transition metals): Valence electrons = electrons in the outermost s-subshell (simplified basic model). In advanced chemistry, both (n−1)d and ns electrons are considered valence electrons.
  • f-block (lanthanides/actinides): Valence electrons = electrons in the outermost s-subshell (basic model). The f-electrons are buried deeper and usually do not participate in bonding at this level.
Valence Electrons (main group) = electrons in the highest occupied shell n.

Valency

Valency is the combining capacity of an element — the number of electrons an atom must lose, gain, or share to attain a stable configuration (usually an octet, or duplet for He).

Rules for Transition Metals (d-block)

Transition metals show variable valency because they can lose electrons from both the outermost s-subshell and the inner (n−1)d-subshell. The game uses the most common/simple school-level valency for each element.

Examples: Fe commonly shows 2 or 3; Cu shows 1 or 2; Mn shows 2, 4, 7; Zn shows 2.

Rules for Inner Transition Metals (f-block)

Lanthanides and actinides generally exhibit a valency of 3 because they typically lose 2 electrons from the 6s/7s subshell and 1 electron from the 4f/5f subshell.

Aufbau Principle — Filling Order

Electrons fill atomic orbitals in order of increasing energy. The sequence is:

1s → 2s → 2p → 3s → 3p → 4s → 3d → 4p → 5s → 4d → 5p → 6s → 4f → 5d → 6p → 7s → 5f → 6d → 7p

Shell Distribution

After writing the configuration, group electrons by principal quantum number n to find the shell-wise distribution:

Example: Fe (Z = 26)
Configuration: 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ 4s² 3d⁶
Shell K (n=1): 2  |  Shell L (n=2): 8  |  Shell M (n=3): 14  |  Shell N (n=4): 2
Valence Shell = 4 (highest n with electrons)
Valence Electrons (basic model) = 2 (4s electrons)
Example: Cl (Z = 17)
Configuration: 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁵
Shell K (n=1): 2  |  Shell L (n=2): 8  |  Shell M (n=3): 7
Valence Shell = 3  |  Valence Electrons = 7  |  Valency = 8 − 7 = 1

Worked Example 1: Neutral Atom — Magnesium (Mg, Z = 12)

Given: Mass Number A = 24
Protons = Z = 12 (always)
Neutrons = A − Z = 24 − 12 = 12
Electrons (neutral) = Z = 12
Configuration: 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s²
Shells: K=2, L=8, M=2
Valence Shell = 3 (highest n)
Valence Electrons = 2
Valency = 2 (loses 2 electrons; valence e⁻ ≤ 4)

Worked Example 2: Cation — Al³⁺ (Aluminum, Z = 13)

Given: A = 27
Protons = Z = 13 (never changes)
Neutrons = 27 − 13 = 14
Charge = +3 → lost 3 electrons
Electrons = 13 − 3 = 10
(Neutral Al has 13 e⁻; Al³⁺ has 10 e⁻)
Valence Shell (of neutral Al) = 3
Valence Electrons (of neutral Al) = 3
Valency = 3

Worked Example 3: Anion — S²⁻ (Sulfide, Z = 16)

Given: A = 32
Protons = Z = 16
Neutrons = 32 − 16 = 16
Charge = −2 → gained 2 electrons
Electrons = 16 + 2 = 18
Configuration of neutral S: 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁴
Valence Shell = 3
Valence Electrons = 6
Valency = 8 − 6 = 2 (gains 2 to complete octet)

Worked Example 4: Transition Metal — Fe²⁺ (Iron, Z = 26)

Given: A = 56
Protons = Z = 26
Neutrons = 56 − 26 = 30
Charge = +2 → lost 2 electrons
Electrons = 26 − 2 = 24
Configuration: 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ 4s² 3d⁶
Valence Shell = 4 (outermost principal shell)
Valence Electrons (basic) = 2 (4s electrons)
Valency = 2 (Fe commonly shows +2 and +3; in this game we use 2 as the primary/simple valency)

Worked Example 5: Noble Gas — Neon (Ne, Z = 10)

Given: A = 20
Protons = 10  |  Neutrons = 10  |  Electrons = 10
Configuration: 1s² 2s² 2p⁶
Valence Shell = 2
Valence Electrons = 8
Valency = 0 (already has a complete octet; does not combine)

Isotopes

Isotopes are atoms of the same element with the same atomic number but different mass number, because their nuclei contain different numbers of neutrons.

Mass Number (A) = Protons + Neutrons   |   Neutrons = A − Z

Isotopes of an element have the same chemical properties (same electron configuration) but different physical properties such as density, melting point, and boiling point.

Carbon isotopes: ₆C¹², ₆C¹³, ₆C¹⁴ (all have 6 protons; neutrons = 6, 7, 8)
Chlorine isotopes: ₁₇Cl³⁵, ₁₇Cl³⁷ (both have 17 protons; neutrons = 18, 20)

Why Atoms Combine

Atoms combine to form molecules to attain chemical stability. They try to achieve the stable electronic configuration of the nearest inert gas — an octet (8 electrons in the outermost shell) or a duplet (2 electrons, like helium).

This is done by gaining, losing, or sharing electrons.

Ions

When an atom loses or gains electrons, the balance of positive and negative charges becomes unequal and a charged particle called an ion is formed.

  • Cation: positively charged ion formed by losing electrons. e.g. Na⁺, Mg²⁺, Fe²⁺.
  • Anion: negatively charged ion formed by gaining electrons. e.g. Cl⁻, O²⁻.
Na → Na⁺ + e⁻  (11 e⁻ → 10 e⁻)    Cl + e⁻ → Cl⁻  (17 e⁻ → 18 e⁻)
Cations have fewer electrons than protons; anions have more electrons than protons.

Radicals

A radical is an atom of an element or a group of atoms of different elements that behaves as a single unit with a positive or negative charge.

  • Basic radicals (cations): Na⁺, NH₄⁺, Cu²⁺, Fe²⁺
  • Acid radicals (anions): Cl⁻, NO₃⁻, SO₄²⁻, CO₃²⁻, PO₄³⁻

Valency

Valency is the combining capacity of an atom or radical — the number of hydrogen atoms it will combine with or displace.

HCl: 1 Cl combines with 1 H → valency of Cl = 1
H₂S: 1 S combines with 2 H → valency of S = 2
H₂SO₄: 1 SO₄ radical combines with 2 H → valency of SO₄ = 2
HNO₃: 1 NO₃ radical combines with 1 H → valency of NO₃ = 1

Variable Valency

Some elements show more than one valency. For metals, the lower valency gets the suffix -ous and the higher valency gets -ic. Modern notation uses Roman numerals.

MetalLower valencyHigher valency
Iron (Fe)Ferrous / Fe(II) / Fe²⁺Ferric / Fe(III) / Fe³⁺
Copper (Cu)Cuprous / Cu(I) / Cu⁺Cupric / Cu(II) / Cu²⁺

For non-metals, the number of other atoms attached determines the valency. Phosphorus forms PCl₃ (valency 3) and PCl₅ (valency 5).

🧬 Isotopes

Isotopes are atoms of the same element with the same atomic number but different mass number because of different numbers of neutrons.

Mass Number (A) = Protons + Neutrons  |  Neutrons = A − Z

Hydrogen Isotopes

Protium ₁H¹: p⁺=1, e⁻=1, n⁰=0
Deuterium ₁H²: p⁺=1, e⁻=1, n⁰=1
Tritium ₁H³: p⁺=1, e⁻=1, n⁰=2

More Examples

Carbon: ₆C¹², ₆C¹³, ₆C¹⁴ (6 protons; 6, 7, 8 neutrons)
Chlorine: ₁₇Cl³⁵, ₁₇Cl³⁷ (17 protons; 18, 20 neutrons)

Key point: Isotopes have the same chemical properties but different physical properties like density, melting point, and boiling point.

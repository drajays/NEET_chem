🗺️ The Aufbau Sequence & Energy Levels

Electrons must fill the lowest available energy levels first before moving upward. The diagram below acts as your roadmap. Notice how the 4s orbital sits lower in energy than the 3d orbital, creating the standard filling gap.

Aufbau Order

1s → 2s → 2p → 3s → 3p → 4s → 3d → 4p → 5s → 4d → 5p → 6s → 4f → 5d → 6p → 7s → 5f → 6d → 7p

Energy Rule

Fill orbitals in increasing order of (n + ℓ). For equal n + ℓ, lower n wins.

🚫 Pauli’s Exclusion Principle

No two electrons in an atom can have the exact same four quantum numbers. Practically, an orbital box can hold at most two electrons, and they must point in opposite directions (↑↓).

📝 Summary Cheat-Sheet

1. Aufbau

Build from the ground up. Energy order: 1s → 2s → 2p → 3s → 3p → 4s → 3d → 4p…

2. Pauli

Use up and down arrows (↑↓) in any single orbital. Never pair two up-arrows (↑↑).

3. Hund

Give every orbital its own electron (↑ | ↑ | ↑) before forcing them to share (↑↓).

🪐 Bohr-Bury Shell Model — KLMNOPQ Rules

Proposed by Niels Bohr & Charles Bury (1921), these four rules explain the periodic table before s,p,d,f subshell theory. They follow directly from the Aufbau principle.

Rule 1 — 2n² Capacity

Maximum electrons in shell n = 2n². K=2, L=8, M=18, N=32 …

Rule 2 — Octet Rule

The outermost (valence) shell can never hold more than 8 electrons (duplet exception for H & He).

Rule 3 — Penultimate Limit

The second-to-last shell ≤ 18 e⁻. The third-from-last ≤ 32 e⁻.

Rule 4 — Step-Wise Filling

A new outer shell begins once the previous hits 8. A shell expands past 8 only after the next shell has ≥ 2 electrons.

⚛️ Section 1 — Quantum Numbers & Orbital Energies

Energy ordering, node counting, and orbital angular momentum.

Q1.Arrange the following orbitals in decreasing order of their energy: 4f, 5p, 5d, 6s.
Use the (n + ℓ) rule — orbitals fill in increasing order of (n+ℓ); for equal (n+ℓ), lower n means lower energy.
Orbitalnℓn + ℓ
4f437
5p516
5d527
6s606

Tie-breaker: for equal (n+ℓ), lower n = lower energy. So 5p (n=5) < 6s (n=6) and 4f (n=4) < 5d (n=5).

Decreasing energy: 5d > 4f > 6s > 5p
Q2.What is the maximum number of electrons in an atom that can have n = 4 and mℓ = 0?
For n = 4 the subshells are 4s, 4p, 4d, 4f. Every subshell has exactly one orbital with mℓ = 0.

Total orbitals with mℓ = 0 = 4 (one in each subshell). By the Pauli Exclusion Principle each orbital holds a maximum of 2 electrons.

4 × 2 = 8 electrons
Q3.Calculate angular and radial nodes for the differentiating electron in Tungsten (W, Z = 74).
Tungsten is a d-block element; its outermost filled orbital per Aufbau is 5d.

Angular nodes = ℓ = 2 (for a d-orbital).
Radial nodes = n − ℓ − 1 = 5 − 2 − 1 = 2.

2 angular nodes and 2 radial nodes
Q4.The orbital angular momentum of an electron in a 5f orbital of Uranium is:
Formula: √ℓ(ℓ+1) · h/2π. For any f orbital, ℓ = 3.

√3(3+1) = √12 = 2√3.

2√3 · h/2π

🎯 Section 2 — Exceptions & Stability Principles

Anomalous configurations, exchange energy, and magnetic moments.

Q5.Assertion (A): Cu configuration is 3d¹⁰ 4s¹ instead of 3d⁹ 4s². Reason (R): Exchange energy is maximized only when half-filled.
Evaluate the Assertion–Reason pair.

Assertion is True: Cu is [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s¹ (not 3d⁹ 4s²).
Reason is False: exchange energy is maximized for both half-filled and completely filled subshells. A full d¹⁰ provides both maximum exchange energy and a highly symmetrical distribution.

A is true, but R is false.
Q6.Calculate the total number of exchange pairs for 3d electrons in Chromium (Z = 24).
Chromium's ground state is [Ar] 3d⁵ 4s¹ — the 3d subshell has 5 parallel-spin electrons.

Exchange pairs = n(n−1)/2 = 5 × 4 / 2 = 10.

10 exchange pairs
Q7.Which transition metal does NOT exhibit an anomalous electronic configuration? (a) Cr (b) Mo (c) W (d) Ag
Compare expected vs actual configurations.
  • Cr (Z=24): 3d⁵ 4s¹ — anomalous
  • Mo (Z=42): 4d⁵ 5s¹ — anomalous (same group)
  • Ag (Z=47): 4d¹⁰ 5s¹ — anomalous (like Cu)
  • W (Z=74): 5d⁴ 6s² — normal, expected
(c) Tungsten — poor 4f shielding and high nuclear charge make the promotion unfavorable.
Q8.Ground state electronic configuration of Palladium (Pd, Z = 46) and its spin-only magnetic moment.
Palladium is a unique exception where both s-electrons shift to the d-subshell.

Configuration: [Kr] 4d¹⁰ 5s⁰. Unpaired electrons = 0.
Spin-only moment: √0(0+2) = 0 BM.

[Kr] 4d¹⁰ 5s⁰ · μ = 0 BM (diamagnetic)

🌐 Section 3 — Lanthanides & Actinides (f-block)

Contraction, half-filled stability, oxidation states, and ionic configurations.

Q9.Statement I: Actinide contraction occurs from Ac to Lr. Statement II: 5f electrons provide superior shielding compared to 4f.
Evaluate the two statements.

Statement I is Correct: actinide contraction is observed.
Statement II is Incorrect: 5f orbitals are more diffuse than 4f, so they shield poorly. This poor shielding lets the nuclear charge pull electrons tighter.

I is correct, II is incorrect.
Q10.Ground state electronic configuration of Gadolinium (Gd, Z = 64).
Expected: [Xe] 4f⁸ 6s². But a half-filled f⁷ subshell is exceptionally stable.

To gain the stability of a half-filled f⁷ subshell, one electron shifts to the 5d orbital.

[Xe] 4f⁷ 5d¹ 6s²
Q11.How many unpaired electrons are present in a neutral Uranium atom (Z = 92)?
Configuration of Uranium: [Rn] 5f³ 6d¹ 7s².

5f³: 3 unpaired electrons (Hund's rule, 7 orbitals).
6d¹: 1 unpaired electron.

Total = 3 + 1 = 4 unpaired electrons
Q12.Why do early actinides (U, Np, Pu) exhibit more oxidation states than early lanthanides?
Compare the energy-level gaps in actinides vs lanthanides.

The 5f, 6d, and 7s levels are comparable (almost degenerate). Because the gaps are so small, electrons from all three can participate in bonding, giving highly variable oxidation states (up to +6 or +7).

Comparable 5f, 6d and 7s energies allow all outer electrons to participate in bonding.
Q13.Write the electronic configuration of the Ce³⁺ ion (Cerium, Z = 58).
Neutral Ce: [Xe] 4f¹ 5d¹ 6s². Remove electrons from the highest principal quantum number first.

Remove 6s² first, then 5d¹.

Ce³⁺: [Xe] 4f¹

🧩 Section 4 — Core Principles & Problem Solving

Rule violations, element identification, ionic stability, and radii.

Q14.If a theoretical atom had 3 electrons in a single 2s orbital, which rule is violated?
A single orbital has only two spin states (ms = +½ and −½).

A third electron would be forced to duplicate all four quantum numbers with one of the others.

Violates Pauli's Exclusion Principle.
Q15.Identify the element whose configuration has K=2, L=8, M=13, N=1.
Total electrons = 2 + 8 + 13 + 1 = 24.
  • K (n=1): 1s² = 2
  • L (n=2): 2s² 2p⁶ = 8
  • M (n=3): 3s² 3p⁶ 3d⁵ = 13
  • N (n=4): 4s¹ = 1

This matches the exceptional [Ar] 3d⁵ 4s¹.

Chromium (Cr)
Q16.Which ion is more stable in aqueous medium: Mn²⁺ or Mn³⁺ (Z = 25)?
Mn (Z=25): [Ar] 3d⁵ 4s². Remove electrons from the highest n first.

Mn²⁺: [Ar] 3d⁵ (exactly half-filled, highly stable).
Mn³⁺: [Ar] 3d⁴ (less stable).

Mn²⁺ is more stable — the half-filled d-subshell maximizes exchange energy and symmetry.
Q17.Identify the lowest atomic number element that possesses a completely filled d-subshell.
A filled d-subshell means d¹⁰. The first d-subshell to fill is 3d.

Expected: Zn (Z=30) → [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s². But due to anomalous stability, Cu (Z=29) steals an electron from 4s to reach [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s¹.

Copper (Z=29) — lowest atomic number with a d¹⁰ configuration.
Q18.Calculate the spin-only magnetic moment of an actinide ion with a 5f³ configuration.
The 5f subshell has 7 degenerate orbitals; by Hund's rule 3 electrons occupy separate orbitals with parallel spins.

Unpaired electrons n = 3. μ = √n(n+2) = √3(5) = √15 ≈ 3.87 BM.

μ ≈ 3.87 BM
Q19.An orbital diagram is drawn as [↑↓][↑][ ]. Which rule does this violate?
The diagram pairs the first p-orbital while the third orbital is still empty.

Hund's rule states that pairing in orbitals of the same subshell cannot occur until each orbital is singly occupied with parallel spins. Correct diagram: [↑][↑][↑].

Violates Hund's Rule of Maximum Multiplicity.
Q20.Arrange the following isoelectronic species in decreasing order of ionic radii: N³⁻, O²⁻, F⁻, Na⁺, Mg²⁺, Al³⁺.
All species have 10 electrons (1s² 2s² 2p⁶). For isoelectronic species, radius decreases as nuclear charge (Z) increases.

Protons: N(7) < O(8) < F(9) < Na(11) < Mg(12) < Al(13). Higher nuclear charge pulls the same electron cloud tighter.

N³⁻ > O²⁻ > F⁻ > Na⁺ > Mg²⁺ > Al³⁺
